# Always firefighting — how can I own a project?

[English](02-firefighting.en.md) | [简体中文](02-firefighting.zh-CN.md) · [Index](README.md)

> Practical heuristics, not scientifically validated universal conclusions. Adapt to authority, relationships, and constraints.

Being repeatedly asked to step in suggests people rely on you, but does not necessarily mean you control your workload. Distinguish real emergencies from unclear ownership or everything being labeled urgent.

## Clarify the constraints

Who sets priorities? Does new work replace old commitments? What uninterrupted time and support would your proposed project need? Without those conditions, volunteering for ownership can simply add workload.

## Two options

**Make the tradeoff explicit:** “I can handle this first. Which of today's commitments, A or B, should move?” This needs someone able to resolve priorities and may involve an uncomfortable conversation.

**Request a bounded piece of ownership:** propose a small project with a goal, deliverable, and deadline, and discuss what existing work it replaces. Do not assume evenings are the solution.

## A small experiment

For the next non-emergency interruption, clarify one priority tradeoff; alternatively, outline the project and time needed on one page. Choose one. Follow the team's incident process during genuine emergencies.

## What to observe

Look for clearer priorities, protected time, and sufficient authority. If the request is declined, understand resource and business constraints before escalating. This approach does not guarantee promotion.

**Try asking:** “I want to own a small project, but interruptions keep taking over. Help me prepare a conversation.”
